What a stroke risk model actually does at its operating point

Most public versions of this project report ~95% accuracy on a dataset where 4.87% of people had a stroke — a number you can beat by predicting "no" every time. This page exists to show the other side of that: what the model gets right, what it costs, and how often it is wrong when it says yes.

What this number is not

  • It is not your risk. It is the rate observed among people with similar values in one dataset of 5,110 records whose origin, country and time period are not published. Nothing about it is calibrated to you.
  • Most of the people it flags did not have a stroke. At the default threshold, roughly — of every 10 flagged people are false alarms. That is not a defect in this particular model — it is what any model faces when the outcome affects about 1 person in 20.
  • The model barely beats age on its own. A logistic regression using nothing but age reaches a ROC-AUC of 0.834; adding the other nine variables moves it to 0.843. Most of what this page reacts to is how old you said you were.
  • The confidence intervals are wide. Only about 50 stroke cases land in the held-out set, so the reported PR-AUC of — carries a 95% interval of roughly 0.15–0.33.
  • Missing BMI is treated as information, not absence. In this dataset the 201 records with no BMI have a 19.9% stroke rate against 4.26% elsewhere — probably an artifact of how the data was collected rather than anything biological. Leave BMI blank and you will see the estimate move for that reason.
